How to get a space job without an aerospace degree

Most people working in space never studied aerospace engineering. Launch providers and satellite makers are manufacturing businesses, software businesses and service businesses at the same time — and they hire accordingly.

Seven proven entry routes

  1. Technician roles. Avionics, composites, harnessing, machining and integration technicians are hired in volume. Certifications such as IPC soldering standards often matter more than degrees.
  2. Software. Ground software, test automation, cloud and data engineering transfer directly from other industries.
  3. Quality and test. Manufacturing quality experience from automotive, medical or electronics is highly transferable.
  4. Supply chain and procurement. Every launch depends on thousands of parts arriving on time.
  5. Mission operations. Operators often come from military, aviation or IT-operations backgrounds.
  6. Business functions. Finance, HR, legal, contracts, marketing and sales all scale with the industry.
  7. Data and geospatial. Earth-observation companies need analysts who can turn imagery into decisions.

The 8 most in-demand space skills in 2026

Industry analyses consistently describe an "experience cliff": plenty of new graduates and senior veterans, but too few mid-career people who have taken a spacecraft from requirements through operations. These are the skills hiring managers struggle to find:

  • Flight software — embedded C/C++, real-time systems, fault management.
  • Systems engineering — requirements, interfaces, verification, MBSE.
  • GNC — guidance, navigation and control for launch vehicles and spacecraft.
  • RF and communications — antennas, link budgets, spectrum.
  • Propulsion — liquid and electric propulsion design and test.
  • Data science and geospatial analytics — ML on imagery and telemetry.
  • Skilled manufacturing — technicians who can build flight hardware to spec.
  • Space security and cyber — protecting satellites and ground networks.

ITAR, clearances and citizenship: what job ads really mean

Space hardware and technical data are often export-controlled. In the US, the International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR) and Export Administration Regulations (EAR) limit who can access certain information.

  • "Must be a US person" usually means a US citizen, lawful permanent resident, or protected individual — required because of ITAR access, not a security clearance.
  • "Ability to obtain a clearance" means the employer will sponsor a government clearance; this generally requires US citizenship.
  • "Active clearance required" means you must already hold one — these roles typically pay a premium.

Outside the US, similar rules exist: ESA roles are generally limited to nationals of member and associate states, and many national programs have their own requirements. Always check before investing time in an application. This is general information, not legal advice.

How to land a space internship

Internships are the single most common path into full-time space roles. Apply early — many summer programs open the previous autumn.

  • Build hardware or code: rocketry clubs, CubeSat teams and SEDS chapters show you can ship.
  • Apply to structured programs: NASA internships, ESA traineeships, and fellowships such as Brooke Owens, Matthew Isakowitz and Patti Grace Smith pair you with mentors.
  • Compete: hackathons like NASA Space Apps and SpaceWorkers contests put your work in front of judges from industry.

Writing a space-industry resume that gets interviews

  • Lead with shipped work: "Designed and flew a 2 kg payload" beats a list of courses.
  • Quantify: mass saved, test hours logged, latency cut, cost reduced.
  • Mirror the job ad's tools: if they use STK, SolidWorks or cFS, say so when you have.
  • State eligibility clearly: citizenship/work status helps screeners on export-controlled roles.
  • Link a portfolio: GitHub, test videos or a one-page project summary.

The space economy in numbers

According to the Space Foundation's July 2026 report, the global space economy grew about 12% to $686 billion in 2025. Commercial activity accounted for roughly $544 billion (79%), while US government space spending was about $78 billion. Lunar activity and in-space servicing were among the fastest-growing segments.

Workforce data point the same way: the Space Foundation reported more than 26,000 jobs added in a single year across the US, Europe, Japan and India, with the UK's space workforce growing 19% year over year. For job seekers, that means more employers, more non-engineering roles and more competition for experienced talent.

Sources: Space Foundation, The State of the Global Space Economy (July 2026); Space Foundation workforce data via Payload (Oct 2024).
